The read

Your boards are already saying one thing, twice

Twenty-three images across Wedding Aesthetic and Design. They split cleanly into two halves that don't look alike at all — and that split is not a problem to resolve. It's the identity.

Half one · the colour is loud

Every florals board is the same jumble: blush and peony pink, peach, coral, butter yellow, powder blue, a lick of lilac — all at once, all on green. Six separate boards name that same family in their own words. Nothing here is a two-colour wedding, and nothing here is muted.

Half two · the paper is quiet

Every single piece in Design is hand-made: loose ink line, watercolour wash, an oval monogram wreath, scalloped and wavy die-cut edges, an illustrated venue, hand-painted stripes, twine bows, olive envelopes. Not one modern sans-serif minimalist suite in the folder.

Green and ivory are the stage. The flowers are the colour. And every printed thing looks drawn by hand, never designed on a screen.

That one sentence is what makes fifteen colours workable instead of chaotic — and it's the sentence every vendor should be handed before anything else.

The anchor

Three words, locked

Every decision in this book answers to these three, which is what makes them worth writing down. Organic was the word we dropped: in vendor language it means beige, muted, undyed linen — the exact word that produced the muted deck you rejected. Say all three out loud to every supplier before you say anything else.

Wild
The un-styled garden abundance, and the Portuguese hillside in June. Everything organic was reaching for, without the beige. It's the word that tells a florist to stop arranging.
Romantic
Your word, and the paper's word: the script logo, watercolour, wreaths, ribbon, a wavy edge on everything. The register the whole suite is written in.
Joyful
The guest-experience word — and the one that stops romantic from turning solemn. It's why butter yellow is the hardest-working accent, and why there are seventy-two doodles.

There's a fourth word governing everything, but it isn't part of the anchor because it's an execution rule, not a feeling: hand-made. Nothing guest-facing should look typeset-only.

The palette

Fifteen colours is a floral palette, not a brand palette

This is the thing to fix. You can't hand fifteen hexes to a printer — they'll pick for you, badly. So the fifteen don't get cut; they get sorted into roles. Two primaries run the brand, three accents lift it, and your logo's rose signs it. The rest live in flowers only.

Primary · the foundation — linens, major florals, stationery grounds

Two, deliberately. A third was tested — butter yellow, then peach — and both were set aside: butter can't be used on ivory at all, and peach sits so close to blush in hue and value that the two merge instead of reading as separate colours. Two primaries means the structure disappears and the flowers do the talking, which is the whole thesis of this book.

Primary 01 Sage Green #A8C29E The one colour on your boards doing a structural job, not a bloom job: the tablecloth, the ribbon, the envelope, André's tie, the sign backdrop. This is the brand's spine.
Primary 02 Blush Pink #F4B6C8 The warm counterweight, and the only pink that appears as a ground in your Design folder — scalloped card borders, envelope liners, napkins. Everything pinker than this is a flower.

Signature · the logo, and nothing else

This one isn't from the fifteen — it's from you. I sampled it out of your logo and your wedding-website header, where it's already doing exactly one job: carrying your names. That's a decision you've made, so it stands.

Signature Antique Rose #D6A794 The wordmark colour. Also correct for the finest details: a hairline rule, a ribbon, a monogrammed handkerchief.
The one thing to watch Antique Rose is a muted, dusty rose — greyer and cooler than Blush Pink #F4B6C8 sitting three rows above it. Held next to each other they read as a mistake rather than a pair, and if the rose spreads onto linens, florals or large printed areas it will quietly drag the whole scheme toward the muted palette you already rejected once. So: fence it in. Antique Rose appears on the wordmark, on hairline rules, and on nothing larger than a ribbon. Everywhere a big soft pink is wanted — napkins, card borders, envelope liners, blooms — use Blush Pink #F4B6C8 instead. Two pinks, two jobs, never touching.

Neutral · paper, ink, metal — three jobs, three answers

You asked for one neutral covering text, metal and negative space. Those pull in different directions, so they each get their own — this is where most suites quietly fall apart.

Ground · negative space Ivory #FAF7F2 Warm cotton paper stock, candles, umbrellas, the dress, table linen under everything. Never optical white — it goes cold against sage.
Ink · all text Evening Fern #4A5A3D Every hand-drawn suite in your Design folder is lettered in green or brown ink — not one uses black. This is the single highest-leverage decision in the book: no black anywhere. It doubles as sage's evening register — deep green cloth on the long tables, with blush and butter florals on top.
Metal Antique Brass #B08D4F Warm and slightly tarnished — the candlesticks, cutlery and frames on your boards are all this. Foil, wax seal rims, cutlery, taper holders. Never silver, never rose gold, never chrome.

The hierarchy, fixed

Names and greetings → the hand. Section headers → Cormorant italic or letterspaced caps. Everything else — details, menus, programmes, times, addresses, signage body — Jost. No exceptions, and no third face creeping in for "just the seating chart".

One thing to change

Your planner and website currently run Fraunces + Nunito + Parisienne. Fraunces is warm and lovely, but its chunky soft serifs crowd watercolour, and Parisienne is a thinner, weaker relative of WindSong. Move to Cormorant Garamond + Jost + WindSong, and place the logo SVG wherever your names appear rather than typing them. Paper is the primary surface here, so the screen should follow it.

Primary mark · vectorised

The M & A wordmark

This is your logo, now a true vector. I traced it from the alpha channel of logo.png — five closed contours, no font involved, nothing to substitute or reflow. It is the same artwork you already have, and it will hold from a 25 mm wax seal to a two-metre welcome sign.

Six files, in Brand/: logo-rose · logo-fern · logo-sage · logo-blush · logo-butter · logo-ivory. Each is one flat colour, so a stationer can recolour it by editing a single value.

One inconsistency to settle. The logo file's own ink is #DE9687; your live website header is #D6A794 — very close, but not the same rose. The artwork is the master, so I've made #DE9687 canonical and would update the site to match. Tell any printer the hex, never "the pink from the website".

Contrast, which is the thing that actually breaks logos: rose and fern both work on ivory. Sage on ivory is too pale to use below display size, and butter on ivory is unusable — that one only sings reversed on fern. Blush needs a fern or ivory ground. When in doubt use fern on ivory; it never fails.

Where it goes: website header · invitation and every card in the suite · welcome sign · menus and programmes · napkin embroidery · favour tags · thank-you cards.

Rules: never stretch it, never re-space the letters, never retype it in a font, never add a shadow or outline, never place it on a busy photograph. Minimum width 32 mm in print. In wax, the hairline connectors will close up — use the seal lockup below at 30 mm or larger and get a proof in real wax before the die is cut.

Secondary lockup

The wordmark in a bloom wreath

The wordmark alone is horizontal, which is right for headers but leaves you nothing for the round, centred moments — the seal, the cake, the dance floor. Two of the eight pieces in your Design folder solve this the same way: a hand-drawn ring broken open by loose blooms. So the wreath doesn't replace your logo, it frames it.

Where it goes: wax seals · envelope back flap · the top cake tier · dance-floor decal · order of service cover · welcome-bag tags · the stamp on thank-you notes.

Repeating texture

The wavy edge & the painted stripe

Four of your eight Design images use a scalloped or wavy border, and three use a hand-painted stripe. Together they're the cheapest possible way to make thirty unrelated items obviously belong to each other — a die-cut edge or a painted wave costs almost nothing and reads instantly across a room.

Wavy edge goes on: RSVP and details cards (die-cut) · menus · place cards · table numbers · the cake tiers · napkin trim · signage frames.

Stripe goes on: envelope liners · the welcome-party invitation · bar-menu backing · favour bags · ribbon. Widths must be uneven and edges soft — painted, not printed. A machine-perfect stripe reads corporate.

Rule: one piece gets a wave or a stripe, never both. The wave belongs to the main day; the stripe to the welcome party and the bar.

The doodles

One pen, drawn loosely on purpose

Eighteen reference sheets now, and they agree completely: a single-weight pen, no shading, no fill, one ink per sheet, a visible wobble — and far more interior detail than a pictogram would carry. Food and drink outnumber everything else, then bows, which appear on almost every sheet. That's a whole design language, and it's the warm, slightly silly counterweight to a very formal script logo.

The whole set was redrawn from scratch against those sheets. The first attempt was built like an icon set — too heavy, too tidy, hollow inside. The line is now 1.9 rather than 2.6, strokes overshoot and don't always meet, and everything has an inside: roof tiles, window panes, drape folds, bottle labels, bubbles in the glass.

It's since grown to 78 drawings in seven groups, plus marks, frames and two small-size variants — 88 files. The flowers came from your botanical sheet, the party group tripled from the doodle folder, and the newest group is the two of you.

On the figures: the six couples are the one group I didn't draw. I attempted them six times — too stiff, then too fussy, then two poses that never resolved at all — and each pass missed. Marie had already made exactly the drawings she wanted, so the right move was to stop drawing and trace them instead, with the same tool that vectorised the wordmark. That's why they're the only doodles here made of filled outlines rather than strokes, and why they're the only ones that look like a person drew them.

Some were cut along the way. Pastel de nata and lemon because you didn't like them; the arch because you didn't love it; a swan, a heeled shoe and two dancing stick figures because they read as a plate, a blob and children's drawings.

All six poses are here, including the bridal carry and the dip — the two I'd previously said were beyond me. They were, by hand. Tracing made the question moot.

One line weight

Every doodle is the same stroke, round-capped — 1.9 on a 100-unit grid, about 1.3 pt printed at 25 mm. No thick-thin, no tapering.

Never mixed weights in one drawing.

Outline only

No fills, no hatching, no shadows. Where colour is wanted, drop a flat wash behind the line, deliberately misregistered by a millimetre or two — the risograph look two of your sheets use.

Never a neatly filled shape.

One ink per piece

A menu is all fern. A bar sign is all coral. Mixing inks across one sheet is the fastest way to make this look like clipart.

Never a multicoloured doodle.

Wobble is the point

Lines don't quite close, circles aren't round, symmetry is always slightly off, and strokes overshoot the corner. It should look drawn in one pass without lifting the pen.

Never a true circle or a ruled straight line.

Draw the inside too

This is what separates illustration from an icon. Roof tiles, panes in every window, folds in the tablecloth, a label on the bottle, bubbles in the glass, scales on the fish.

Never an empty silhouette.

Grouped so you can find things: a stationer designing a bar sign shouldn't have to scroll past the flowers. All in Brand/doodles/, named to match these captions.

Frames · the wavy edge, drawn

Where they go

Menus get food doodles down the margins, one ink. The itinerary card gets the house, a coupe, the cake and the disco ball beside the times. Table numbers sit inside the wobbly rectangle. Bar signs get the bottle and the olives. Favour tags get one doodle each. Welcome-bag cards get a scattering of marks. The order of service gets the swallows. Any label beside a doodle is Jost letterspaced caps — never a script.

How not to overdo it

The doodles are seasoning, not the meal. Three doodles per piece is plenty; six is too many. Never put a doodle next to the watercolour of the house on the same piece — the loose pen and the painted illustration fight each other. And never let a doodle touch the wordmark: your logo is formal and high-contrast, and the pen undercuts it at close range. Give them separate corners.

The rules

Seven lines that turn a mood into a brand

This is the page to paste into an email when a vendor sends a proof. Every one of them comes straight off your own boards — and each has a matching "never", because that's what actually holds a look together across fourteen suppliers.

Ink is green

All text, on everything, in Evening Fern #4A5A3D — paper, signage, website body.

Never black. Not for "just the small print".

The wordmark is artwork

Send the SVG from Brand/. Greetings beyond it are hand-lettered or set in WindSong — never retyped in the logo's own script.

Never retyped, restretched, re-spaced or re-coloured off-palette.

Every piece has one hand-made edge

A scalloped die-cut, a painted wave, a deckled edge, or a hand-torn side.

Never four clean machine-trimmed edges.

Colour arrives as flowers, not fields

Maximum two flat colour areas per printed piece. Everything brighter comes in as a painted bloom.

Never a full-bleed pink or yellow card.

Green and ivory hold the room

Linens, envelopes, ribbon, glassware, attire for André and the men: sage, fern, ivory. Let the flowers be the loud thing.

Never pastel linen and pastel florals on the same table — it goes to mush.

Metal is warm brass

Cutlery, candlesticks, frames, foil, seal rims. Glass is clear or ribbed.

Never silver, rose gold, chrome, or matte black.

Shapes are round

Ovals, scallops, circles, bows, curved arches, round tables, coupe glasses.

Never sharp geometry, hard rectangles, or a straight-line arch.
